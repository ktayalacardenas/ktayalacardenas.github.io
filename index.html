Helping build a behavioral testing system

During my summer research fellowship, I worked on hardware for an automated rodent behavioral testing platform. I assembled and soldered supplied PCBs, checked connections and voltages with a digital multimeter, and helped integrate infrared beam-break sensors, servos, an LCD, and Arduino control.

Modeled Neural Pathways

Diagram of the modeled pathways, not biological axons.

UAA · McNair Scholars · 2025–2026

What happens when a modeled signal takes a different route?

For my independent McNair research, I built an Arduino-based system using two four-node LED pathways and a servo output. The system modeled how rerouting could affect simulated neural signal transmission under different levels of impairment.

I developed the experiment, compared rerouting locations, and measured reliability across 1,457 trials. In the severe-impairment condition, late rerouting raised the model's successful-output rate from approximately 14% to 75%.

This was a physical engineering model, not a test on biological axons. It taught me how to turn an open-ended question into a testable system, define outcomes, and conduct enough trials to evaluate performance. I presented the findings at the 2026 UAA Student Showcase.

Washington University in St. Louis · 2026

Moving from neural signals to questions about behavior

At WashU, I also worked with 48-channel neural recordings, using MATLAB and Python workflows for spike detection and sorting, waveform features, firing-rate analysis, and principal component analysis.

I trained a DeepLabCut model to identify anatomical landmarks in rodent reaching videos. Seeing both sides, the physical behavioral setup and the neural and movement data, made me more interested in how engineers connect measurements to what an animal is actually doing.

What I learned

  • How neural recordings can be processed into interpretable features.
  • Why behavioral context matters when analyzing neural activity.
  • Why validation is important before trusting an analysis result.

UAA · Electronics Design Lab · Spring 2026

Designing, building, and measuring an analog PWM circuit

For an electronics project, I designed a 12-V analog PWM LED controller using an RC timing circuit, comparators, and transistor drivers. I tested the design in LTspice and participated in breadboard assembly and troubleshooting.

We evaluated the physical circuit using an oscilloscope, measuring an oscillation frequency of approximately 0.72 Hz and an output close to 11.94 V peak-to-peak.

Debugging the transistor orientation reinforced an important lesson for me: when a circuit doesn't behave as expected, return to the measurements and investigate the possible causes instead of assuming the design is working correctly.
